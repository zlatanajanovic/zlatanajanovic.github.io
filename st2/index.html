Sequentially Teaching Sequential Tasks (ST)2: Teaching Robots Long-horizon Manipulation Skills

(ST)2 lets a human teacher split a long-horizon task into segments while demonstrating it, so the robot can be taught, corrected, and re-taught one step at a time.

Abstract

Learning from demonstration has proved itself useful for teaching robots complex skills with high sample efficiency. However, teaching long-horizon tasks with multiple skills is challenging as deviations tend to accumulate, the distributional shift becomes more evident, and human teachers become fatigued over time, thereby increasing the likelihood of failure.

To address these challenges, we introduce (ST)2, a sequential method for learning long-horizon manipulation tasks that allows users to control the teaching flow by specifying key points, enabling structured and incremental demonstrations. Using this framework, we study how users respond to two teaching paradigms: (i) a traditional monolithic approach, in which users demonstrate the entire task trajectory at once, and (ii) a sequential approach, in which the task is segmented and demonstrated step by step.

We conducted an extensive user study on the restocking task with 16 participants in a realistic retail store environment, evaluating the user preferences and effectiveness of the methods. User-level analysis showed superior performance for the sequential approach in most cases (10 users), compared with the monolithic approach (5 users), with one tie. Our subjective results indicate that some teachers prefer sequential teaching, as it allows them to teach complicated tasks iteratively, while others prefer teaching in one go due to its simplicity.

(ST)2: Sequentially Teaching Sequential Tasks

Kinesthetic teaching is the most common way to demonstrate a manipulation skill, but for a long-horizon task it is physically tiring and mentally demanding: the teacher has to think several steps ahead while guiding the arm, and the quality of the demonstration drops as fatigue builds up. Monolithic trajectories are also prone to error accumulation and distributional shift, and a small mistake late in the demonstration can mean redoing everything.

(ST)2 gives the teacher explicit control over the teaching flow. The task T = {T1, …, Tm} is demonstrated as a sequence of user-defined segments: the teacher inserts a key-point whenever a sub-goal is reached (a top grasp, a collision-free reach, a placement, a gripper release), and a separate SIMPLe policy (a time-dependent Gaussian-process policy with an uncertainty-aware Cartesian impedance controller) is learned for each segment. The robot then executes the segments in sequence, and the teacher can replay the segments that already work, take over at any key-point, and provide a new demonstration from there. Five high-level commands, issued by spoken language, steer the process:

  • c_demoSwitch to demonstration mode: the arm becomes compliant and the teacher guides it kinesthetically while the segment is recorded.
  • c_insertKPInsert a key-point: close the current segment, add it to the dataset, and start a new one.
  • c_nextExecute the current policy autonomously until the next key-point; kinesthetic corrections during execution are appended to the segment.
  • c_resetReturn the robot and the environment to the start of the episode.
  • c_autoExecute the complete task autonomously by running the segment policies one after another.

After every interaction the policies are retrained on the updated dataset, which takes a few seconds on a laptop without a GPU. Because a new demonstration always starts from the end point of the previous segment, no extra machinery is needed to make the transitions between segments smooth, and the teacher can change the granularity of the segmentation at any time by overwriting a segment and everything after it.

Supermarket Restocking Task

The user study uses a real-world restocking task in a mock retail store: a Franka Emika Panda has to move a milk or yogurt carton from a storage box to a shelf. The box only allows a top grasp, but a top-grasped carton cannot be placed on the upper shelf, so the robot has to put the carton down on the table, re-grasp it from the side, and only then move it to the shelf. The task is long enough to make the differences between monolithic and sequential teaching visible, while remaining safe and reproducible across participants.

The task was analysed with Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) principles before the study. We identified sixteen error types across teaching (e.g., touching other objects, reaching joint limits, non-perpendicular placing), task planning (skipping the re-grasp, placing at the reachability limit), segmenting (too few or meaningless key-points), correcting (missing the moment to correct), and setup (gripper malfunctions). Their frequencies during the study are reported in the results.

User Study

Sixteen participants (3 female, 13 male, 22–40 years old) took part; 14 had no prior experience with learning from demonstration and 9 had never interacted with a robot. Each session consisted of a 15-minute familiarisation with the compliant robot and both teaching methods, a 30-minute experimental session in which the participant taught the restocking task with the monolithic method and with (ST)2 in random order (up to three attempts from scratch per method), and a 20-minute questionnaire session with the NASA Task Load Index and open-ended questions. The study was approved by the TU Delft Human Research Ethics Committee.

RQ1: Is the (ST)2 (sequential) method more user-friendly or effective than the monolithic method?

RQ2: Under what conditions does one teaching method become preferable over the other?

Our hypothesis was that users who manage to demonstrate the whole task in one attempt would prefer the monolithic method for its simplicity and speed, while users whose robot fails to reproduce the demonstration reliably would shift toward the sequential approach, which offers more control and more opportunities to correct.

User preferences

Users were asked which method they preferred regarding comfort, trust, expectations, and their perception of the robot's understanding of the task. Preferences are clearly split between the two methods, yet most users declared being comfortable with, and trusting, both of them. A notable share of users favoured (ST)2 for trust, met expectations, and the feeling that the robot understood the task.

Subjective workload (NASA-TLX)

The monolithic method was rated as less physically demanding (overall p = 0.01), which users attributed to the single in-one-go demonstration. (ST)2 was perceived as less temporally demanding (overall p = 0.03): with the monolithic policy, users spent a lot of time correcting, since finding the right moment to start and stop a correction proved hard for non-experts. Although one could expect a higher mental demand from having to decide where to put key-points, no significant difference in mental demand was found (p = 0.42), and there were no significant differences in perceived performance, effort, or frustration.

“It felt more satisfying getting the robot to perform the complete ‘choreography’ correctly in a single movement.” User #03, on the monolithic method

“Potentially, I would have to re-demonstrate everything to the robot if it fails.” User #16, on the monolithic method

“This task consists of sub-tasks. So I think I am more comfortable as a demonstrator to achieve the final goal if I can teach tasks separately. Also, I do not need to consider the optimization of the whole task, which reduces the need to think.” User #02, on (ST)2

“Yes, it allowed me to ‘explain’ the task in segments, which is also how I prefer to do demonstrations to humans. Also, a small mistake felt less ‘stressful’.” User #07, on (ST)2

Task performance

Task performance is scored as 1 / #trials, the inverse of the number of attempts needed for a successful execution. Over all users, the scores of the two methods are not significantly different (p = 0.98), with success rates of 51.1 % for the monolithic method and 65.5 % for (ST)2. At the user level, 10 users did better with the sequential approach, 5 with the monolithic approach, and one tied. We attribute this to the teaching flow: (ST)2 lets users focus their corrections on the problematic segment only, so a successful task is reached in fewer runs.

“I prefer the (ST)2 method because I was able to correct just after a certain step. If everything was good up to that point, I could ‘save my work’.” User #05, on (ST)2

Quality of the learned policies

Policies learned with (ST)2 produce smoother trajectories, both in Cartesian space and in the commanded torques, while completing the task in about the same time and with a higher success rate. This supports the assumption that teachers give better demonstrations and corrections when they control the segmentation.

Learned behaviour

The mean trajectories and their variability across users are similar for both methods, which shows that the task design leaves participants freedom in how they segment while keeping the resulting policies comparable, so the two frameworks can be compared directly.

Comparison against automatic segmentation

Automatic segmentation methods need a complete demonstration before they can segment it, and they cluster points by proximity without a guaranteed meaning for the teacher. In the example below, a demonstration by user #16 is segmented automatically with a physically-consistent dynamical-systems method; the light-blue segment mixes points from fetching the carton with points from placing it on the shelf, which would be ambiguous for a policy trained on it. The key-points set by the user, on the other hand, closely follow the sub-task boundaries of the task breakdown.

FMEA error frequencies

A large share of trials contained at least one of the errors identified in the FMEA analysis (76 % for monolithic, 54.5 % for (ST)2), which confirms that the task is challenging enough for the study. Errors involving other objects or several sub-tasks occurred more often with the monolithic method; with (ST)2, users reached the joint limits more often, which we attribute to disregarding the path taken between segments.

Takeaways

  • Letting the teacher segment a long-horizon task on the fly reduces the perceived temporal demand, because corrections can be focused on the segment that failed instead of the whole demonstration.
  • Preferences are split and did not align with task success: some users value the control of sequential teaching, others the simplicity of a single demonstration. Perceived usability and actual performance can diverge, so both need to be measured.
  • (ST)2 policies are smoother than monolithic ones, with the same execution time and a higher success rate at the user level.
  • Future work: alternative segmentation interfaces, a broader set of tasks of varying length and complexity, and long-term studies of how teaching strategies evolve as users gain experience.
